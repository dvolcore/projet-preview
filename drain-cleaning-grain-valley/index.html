Grain Valley housing and what it means for your pipe

A young town, which changes what goes wrong.

Grain Valley grew up late and fast. Census American Community Survey estimates put the median year a Grain Valley home was built at 2002, with roughly 57 percent of the housing stock going up in 2000 or later and barely two percent predating 1950. That makes this one of the only cities in the eastern metro where the sewer lateral under your yard is almost certainly PVC rather than clay tile or cast iron.

PVC fails differently. It does not scale up or rust through, so the classic cast-iron symptom of a drain that slows a little every year is rare here. What we find instead is bellied line where backfill settled under a driveway, root hair pushed in through a rubber gasket at a joint, and grease that has nothing rough to catch on until it reaches a sag and stops. A cable can punch through all three. Only a camera tells you which one you actually have, and whether it will be back in six months.

The newer the pipe, the more the yard matters. Grain Valley's build-out ran straight through the 2004 to 2007 permit peak, when the city approved well over a hundred single-family permits a year, so a lot of laterals were laid in the same few seasons, in the same trench conditions, under lawns that are only now mature enough to send roots looking for water.

Where we work in Grain Valley

Older pockets along Main Street and the original town grid sit on a different vintage of pipe than Grayleigh Park or Cross Creek, and we treat them differently.

The city owns the collection mains here, but every drop of Grain Valley wastewater is treated at the Blue Springs-owned Sni-A-Bar plant northeast of town, and the city has budgeted sanitary sewer upgrades specifically to cut inflow and infiltration from deteriorated and damaged mains.
