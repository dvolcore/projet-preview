Cast Iron Drain Pipe Failure: Signs and Options

Cast iron drain pipe fails from the inside, and it fails unevenly. A run under a slab can be structurally gone at the bottom while the top half still looks like pipe. That is why age alone is not a verdict and a camera is.

Where you find it

Cast iron was the standard drain, waste and vent material in American houses from roughly the 1920s until plastic took over in the 1970s. If the house is from that era and the stack is heavy, black, and sounds dull rather than hollow when tapped, it is cast iron.

How it fails

Sanitary drainage is not full-flow piping. Liquid runs along the bottom of the pipe, the invert, while the top of the pipe carries sewer gas. That gas contains hydrogen sulfide. In the thin moist film on the pipe crown, bacteria oxidise hydrogen sulfide into sulfuric acid. So a horizontal cast iron drain is attacked by acid at the top and by constant wetting and abrasion at the bottom.

Corrosion products build up on the wall as tuberculation, rough nodular deposits bonded to the metal. Two things follow. The pipe loses diameter, and the wall becomes rough, so paper and solids catch where they used to pass. The line then starts clogging at a rate that has nothing to do with what the household puts down it.

Eventually the invert thins and goes through. In a vertical stack that shows as pinholes, rust bubbles and weeping. In a horizontal run, particularly one buried under a slab, the bottom of the pipe disappears over a length and the pipe becomes an open trough sitting in soil. Plumbers call it channelling or bottom rot. It is the reason a cast iron line can pass a casual look and still be structurally finished.

The signs

  • Recurring clogs in a house where nothing about the household changed.
  • Rust-coloured staining, damp or efflorescence at the base of a stack.
  • Rust bubbles, flaking or a rough surface on exposed pipe.
  • Sewer odour in a basement or crawl space with no dry trap to explain it. See sewer smell in the house.
  • Soft or sunken flooring, or persistent damp, over a slab-embedded run.
  • Hard rust-coloured flakes coming back on a drain cable.
  • On camera: nodular orange-brown buildup, a visibly rough wall, or no identifiable invert at all.

What the camera settles

One question: is this a dirty pipe or a failed pipe? Scale inside a pipe whose wall is still sound can be removed, and the line goes back to service. A pipe with no invert cannot be cleaned into working condition, because there is nothing left to clean. A camera inspection is the only way to tell those apart, and self-levelling matters here specifically, because you have to be able to see the bottom of the pipe.

The options

  • Descaling and jetting. Removing the scale restores diameter and a smooth wall where the metal is still sound. This is routine work on cast iron, and the camera pass afterwards is what tells you whether the wall held. See hydro jetting.
  • Replacement. For sections with channelling, holes or a missing invert. Buried and under-slab runs mean excavation, which we do ourselves. See sewer line repair and replacement.

What does not help: chemical drain cleaner. Caustic and acid products attack the metal you are trying to preserve, and on a pipe that is already thin at the invert that is the opposite of a repair.

About lifespan numbers

Published service life figures for cast iron vary widely, because what actually determines it is waste chemistry, how wet the run stays, soil conditions and how well it was installed. A 70-year-old stack in a dry wall may be in better condition than a 45-year-old buried run under a slab. Age tells you to look. The camera tells you what you have. Questions

Cast Iron Pipe Failure FAQ

Can cast iron be cleaned, or does it have to be replaced?
It depends on the wall, not the age. Scale in a pipe with sound metal can be removed and the line restored. A section with channelling or holes at the invert has to be replaced.
Will hydro jetting damage cast iron?
Jetting sound cast iron is standard practice and is how scale gets removed. On a pipe already corroded through, aggressive cleaning can open the defect, which is why the camera comes first.
Does the age of my cast iron mean I need to replace it?
No. Condition varies enormously with how wet the run stays, soil and installation quality. Age is a reason to inspect, not a diagnosis.
Is cast iron under a concrete slab a problem?
Buried and under-slab runs tend to fail first, because they stay wet and corrosion concentrates at the bottom of the pipe. They are also the runs where failure is least visible until something backs up.
