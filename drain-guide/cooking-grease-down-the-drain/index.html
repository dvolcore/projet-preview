Cooking Grease Down the Drain: Where It Actually Goes

Grease does not leave your house. It changes phase a few feet into the pipe and stays there. Every method people use to make it leave — more hot water, dish soap, running the disposal, chasing it with cold water — moves the deposit to a different part of the line rather than removing it.

Follow the route

A cup of turkey fat poured into a kitchen sink travels through four distinct environments, and it behaves differently in each one.

It leaves the pan at frying temperature and hits the sink basket, where it is still a thin liquid. It drops through the trap, which holds a few inches of standing water, and the first cooling happens there. It enters the branch drain, typically one and a half to two inches across, running at a slight fall across the joists. This is where most of the damage is done: the pipe wall is at room temperature or below, the fat is spread thin across a lot of surface, and it crosses its congealing point within a few feet. Whatever survives that reaches the building drain and then the lateral — the buried pipe between your house and the city main, which belongs to you and which, in Kansas City in late November, is cold.

The key fact is that animal fat is solid at room temperature. It does not need to get cold to set. It only has to stop being hot.

Why the deposit is permanent without mechanical removal

Fresh fat on a pipe wall could, in principle, be re-melted. It usually does not get the chance. Dissolved minerals in hard water and the alkaline residue from dish detergent convert the fat into metallic soaps, and the resulting solid is harder, waxier and far higher-melting than the fat you poured in. The full chemistry is in clogged kitchen sink from grease. KC Water describes the end state plainly through its own staff: the grease hardens as it cools, adheres to the walls, narrows the pipe and traps solids, and it will block a private line before it ever reaches the main.

Once that happens, hot water at household temperature does nothing. What is on the wall is no longer the substance you poured in.

The four things people do instead, and what each one really accomplishes

  • Chase it with lots of hot water. This works, in the sense that the fat leaves the sink. It relocates the deposition zone further down the line, into the buried lateral, where it is harder and more expensive to reach. You have traded a problem under the sink for a problem under the yard.
  • Add dish soap. Detergent emulsifies fat into suspended droplets. The emulsion is temporary; it breaks as the water cools and dilutes, and the droplets coalesce and deposit anyway. The detergent residue then contributes to the soap-forming reaction above. KC Water says directly that hot water or soap does not help.
  • Run cold water so it solidifies and breaks up. This is the least bad of the four, and it is genuinely better than hot water, because solid fat travelling as particles is less likely to coat the wall than liquid fat travelling as a film. It is still putting fat into a two-inch pipe.
  • Run the disposal. No effect whatsoever on fat. A disposal grinds solids; grease is not a solid when it goes in.

What to do with it instead

Pour cooled fat into a can, a jar or the container the oil came in, put a lid on it, and put it in the trash. KC Water's own advice to Kansas City households is to freeze leftover grease and throw it away, and the city's Household Hazardous Waste Facility at 4707 Deramus Avenue takes containers of cooking oil. For the film left in the pan, a paper towel before washing removes more grease than any amount of running water will.

Large volumes of frying oil should never go into a residential drain under any circumstance. Keep the container, let it cool, and dispose of it as oil.

The part that is not your problem, and the part that is

The lateral between your house and the city main is yours. A blockage in it is your bill, not the utility's, which is why wall deposit in the lateral is the expensive version of this problem. The EPA lists blockages among the causes of sanitary sewer overflows, and grease is the blockage material that homeowners have the most direct control over. Decomposing grease deposits also generate sulfides in a sewer, which is part of why a chronically greasy line smells the way it does — see sewer smell in the house if odor is the symptom you started with.

Removing a deposit that is already there

A cable machine bores a channel through a wall deposit. Flow returns, the sink drains, and the coating is still there, so the channel closes again on roughly the same schedule each time. Hydro jetting uses a reverse-facing nozzle at up to 4,000 PSI aimed back against the pipe wall, which shears the deposit off and flushes it out, restoring full diameter. Questions

Cooking Grease FAQ

Can I pour grease down the drain if I run hot water with it?
No. Hot water carries the fat past the trap and it sets again further down the line, usually in the buried lateral where it is harder to reach. KC Water states that pouring grease down the drain with hot water or soap does not help.
Does dish soap break grease down?
Only temporarily. Detergent emulsifies fat into droplets, but the emulsion breaks as the water cools and dilutes, and the fat deposits anyway. Detergent residue also feeds the reaction that turns fat into a hard soap on the pipe wall.
Where should turkey fat and cooking oil go?
Into a can or jar with a lid, then into the trash. KC Water advises Kansas City households to freeze leftover grease and throw it away, and the city's Household Hazardous Waste Facility at 4707 Deramus Avenue accepts containers of cooking oil.
Why does the clog come back a few months after the line is snaked?
Because snaking opens a channel through the deposit rather than removing it. The coating is still on the pipe wall and closes the channel again, usually faster each time as the remaining layer thickens.
