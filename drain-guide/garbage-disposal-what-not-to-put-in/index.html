What Never Goes In a Garbage Disposal, and Why Grinding Does Not Help

A garbage disposal is a grinder sitting above the trap. It makes food waste smaller. It does not make food waste drain-safe. Everything it grinds still has to be carried through a one-and-a-half to two inch branch line by nothing but moving water, and some foods behave worse after grinding than before.

What a disposal actually does

There are no blades in a disposal. A spinning plate throws food against a stationary shredder ring and tears it into particles, then water carries the particles out through the trap and into the branch drain. That is the whole machine. It has no effect on grease, no effect on starch chemistry, and only a partial effect on long plant fibres. Once the slurry leaves the trap it is subject to one thing: whether the pipe has enough slope, diameter and flow to carry it to the main.

This matters because the two most common holiday mistakes are both reasonable-sounding. Running more water seems like it should help. Grinding food finer seems like it should help. Neither changes the thing that causes the clog.

Never, under any circumstances

Fats, oils and grease. This is not a plumber's preference, it is the disposal manufacturer's own instruction. InSinkErator says never to put any type of fat, oil or grease in a garbage disposal, or down a sink drain at all, because they collect inside the plumbing pipes and are the usual cause of plumbing problems. Turkey drippings, gravy, roasting pan residue, bacon fat, butter, cream sauce and salad dressing all count. They go into the pipe hot and liquid, and they leave the pipe as a coating.

Turkey bones and fruit pits. Some higher-end units are rated to grind small bones and pits, so the motor may well survive it. The pipe is the problem. Bone fragments are hard, angular and dense, they do not dissolve, and they settle out of suspension the moment flow slows down. In a line with a low spot or a belly they sit there and give everything soft something to build on.

Fibrous vegetables. Celery, asparagus, artichoke leaves, corn husks, onion skins, rhubarb and the stringy pulp from a pumpkin are long cellulose strands. Shredding shortens them but does not break the fibre down, and the strands wrap around the shredder ring on the way through and travel as mats on the other side. A mat catches on any roughness in the pipe, and roots or scale make the line nothing but roughness.

Starchy food in quantity. Potato peels, mashed potato, stuffing, rice and pasta absorb water and swell. Ground fine and mixed with water they make a paste, and that paste is the perfect binder for a fat layer that is already on the pipe wall. A spoonful is nothing. A colander of peels from twelve pounds of potatoes is a plug.

What the manufacturer permits, with a caveat

Coffee grounds and eggshells are the classic argument, and the honest answer is that InSinkErator says neither is harmful to the disposal and both will pass through it without a problem. That is true of the machine. The caveat is the manufacturer's own: in older plumbing, or a line that has not been cleared in years, too much at once can still cause a clog or a slow drain. Grounds and shell fragments do not stick to clean pipe. They absolutely stick to a pipe that is already coated with fat. Feed them in gradually, with plenty of cold water, and do not tip a whole filter basket in at once.

Cold water, not hot

Hot water is the instinct and it is the wrong one. Hot water melts fat so it slips past the trap, then the water cools in the buried line and the fat sets further downstream, in a section nobody can reach with a sink plunger. KC Water makes the same point directly: pouring grease down the drain with hot water or soap does not help, because the grease settles out again once the water has gone.

Run a strong flow of cold water, start it before the disposal, keep it running for fifteen to twenty seconds after the grinding noise stops, and feed waste in a handful at a time rather than packing the chamber. Cold water keeps fat solid, so what little gets in travels as a particle instead of a film.

The order of operations that actually works

  • Pour cooled fat and oil into a can or jar, lid it, and put it in the trash.
  • Wipe greasy pans and plates with a paper towel before they ever reach the sink.
  • Scrape plates into the trash or the compost. The disposal is for what is left after scraping, not instead of scraping.
  • Keep a basket strainer in the drain and empty it into the bin.
  • Run cold water through the disposal for a few seconds before and after every use.

If it is already clogging

A kitchen line that clogs on a schedule is telling you the wall is coated, not that you were unlucky. Snaking opens a channel through the deposit and the channel closes again. Hydro jetting shears the deposit off the wall and brings the line back to full diameter, which is the difference between a fix measured in months and one measured in years. Questions

Garbage Disposal FAQ

Can I put turkey bones in a garbage disposal?
Keep bones out. Some units are rated to grind small bones, but bone fragments are dense and angular, they do not dissolve, and they settle out in any low spot in the drain line where soft waste then builds on them.
Are potato peels bad for a garbage disposal?
In quantity, yes. Starch swells with water and grinds into a paste, and that paste binds readily to any fat already coating the pipe wall. Peels from a holiday-sized batch of potatoes belong in the trash or the compost.
Should I run hot or cold water with the disposal?
Cold. Hot water melts fat so it passes the trap and then sets further down the line where it cannot be reached. Cold water keeps fat solid so it leaves as a particle.
Are coffee grounds and eggshells safe?
InSinkErator says neither harms the disposal and both pass through it, but warns that too much at once can still clog older plumbing. In a line already coated with grease they settle into the deposit, so feed them in gradually with cold water.
