Why a multi-unit building fails differently

A house has one set of fixtures feeding one line. A stacked building has many fixtures feeding a vertical stack, and the stack feeds a horizontal building drain that feeds one lateral out to the city. Every one of those joins is a place where a restriction stops being local.

Three consequences follow, and they drive everything else:

  • The blockage and the damage are in different units. Water comes out at the lowest opening below the restriction, which is a ground-floor tub, a laundry room or a basement floor drain. The tenant who caused it is often dry and the tenant who calls you is not. Our guide on basement floor drains backing up covers why the relief point is the symptom and not the problem.
  • Load is cumulative and never pauses. A house has a peak. A building has a peak every morning. Grease, hair, wipes and paper from dozens of households accumulate on the same pipe wall continuously.
  • You cannot educate your way out of it. Tenant notices help at the margin. Turnover undoes them. The line still has to be cleaned.

The first diagnostic question is always the same one as in a house, just with higher stakes: is this one fixture or the shared line? Main line clog versus single drain clog is the test, and getting it wrong in a building means clearing a tenant's sink three times while the building drain keeps narrowing.

What preventative service actually prevents

Being specific here matters, because "preventative maintenance" gets sold as though it prevents everything. It does not.

It does prevent deposit-driven failures: grease and soap buildup narrowing a line until an ordinary morning overloads it, scale closing down an old cast iron stack, and root mats rebuilding in a lateral between visits. These are the failures that are a function of time since last cleaning, and a schedule is exactly the right tool for them.

It does not prevent structural failure. A belly, an offset joint, a crack, a collapsed section of clay tile, a cast iron pipe that has lost its bottom. No cleaning interval fixes a pipe that has lost its shape. What a schedule does with those is find them earlier, on a Tuesday, with a camera, instead of on a Sunday night with three units offline. Then it becomes a planned sewer line repair or replacement with a budget and a date.

That distinction is also the honest answer to "we cleaned it last quarter, why did it back up?" Sometimes the answer is that the interval is too long. Sometimes the answer is that the pipe is broken and cleaning was never going to be the fix. The camera is what tells the two apart.

Building the schedule from evidence

A credible program has four steps, and the first one is not cleaning.

  1. Camera the shared lines and record a baseline. Camera inspection with a locator, so every finding has a footage distance and a depth. This is also when you find out whether the building has usable cleanouts, which is worth knowing before an emergency.
  2. Clean to full diameter. Hydro jetting scours the wall rather than punching through the deposit. On older cast iron, descaling comes with it.
  3. Camera again and set the interval. Re-inspect after a period and measure how much came back. That rate, per building, is your schedule. Same logic as how often a drain line should be cleaned, applied to a property instead of a house.
  4. Keep the documentation. Written findings and footage per visit, per line, so an owner, a board or an insurer can see what was done and when.

Two scheduling notes from the field. Turnover is the cheapest time to work a unit's branch line, because nobody is displaced. And the building drain and lateral are best done when the building is quiet, which usually means early. Both are normal parts of a commercial maintenance program, which runs monthly or quarterly depending on what the camera says.

What to hand an owner

If you manage for someone else, the maintenance is only half the job; the other half is being able to show it. Ask for the same package every visit: the video, a written note per defect with footage and depth, the location marked, and a clear cleaning-or-repair recommendation. It answers a capital-expense question before it gets asked, and it is the same documentation that makes a sale or a refinance go smoothly later. The real estate page covers that side.

Questions

Property Manager Drain Maintenance FAQ

How often should a multi-unit building have its drains cleaned?
Set it from the building, not from a rule. We camera the shared lines, clean them to full diameter, then camera again later and measure how much deposit returned. That rate is the interval. A high-occupancy building with old cast iron and a low-occupancy building with PVC do not belong on the same schedule.
Is it cheaper to maintain on a schedule than to call when something backs up?
We will not quote you a saving we cannot prove. What we can say plainly is what each option buys. Scheduled work happens at a known time, in a known unit, with nobody displaced. An emergency happens at the worst hour, usually at the lowest fixture, and often with water damage in a unit that did not cause it.
Can you work around tenants?
Yes. Branch lines are easiest during a turnover, and shared lines are usually done early when the building is quiet. We schedule around occupancy rather than through it.
Will a maintenance program stop every backup?
No, and anyone who says otherwise is selling. A schedule prevents the failures caused by buildup. It does not fix a pipe that has lost its shape. What it does with those is find them with a camera on a planned visit instead of at 2am.
Do you handle the repair as well as the cleaning?
Yes. Drain cleaning, hydro jetting, camera inspection, sewer line repair and full replacement with excavation, all by Pro Jet. Licensed, bonded and insured for residential and commercial work.
