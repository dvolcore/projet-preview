What grease actually does in a line

Warm fats and oils leave a kitchen sink as a liquid and stop being one a few feet in. As the water cools, the fat separates and sticks to the pipe wall, and because it is lighter than water it builds first at the top and sides of a horizontal line. What grows is not a plug. It is a layer, and it keeps growing inward.

That is why the symptom is so misleading. A line with a thick grease coating still drains, slowly, right up until a single service puts enough solids through to bridge the remaining gap. The pipe did not fail that night. It had been failing since the last cleaning. Our guide on where cooking grease goes covers the phase change in detail, and clogged kitchen sinks from grease covers why hot water and chemicals do not reverse it.

An interceptor protects the sewer, not your line

A grease interceptor or trap exists to keep fats, oils and grease out of the public sewer, and that is a real obligation. Federal pretreatment rules prohibit discharging "solid or viscous pollutants in amounts which will cause obstruction to the flow in the POTW resulting in Interference" (40 CFR 403.5(b)(3)), and local wastewater authorities run their own grease programs on top of that.

What an interceptor does not do is protect the pipe between your fixtures and the interceptor. Everything upstream of it still carries hot grease-laden water, and that is the run that coats. Kitchens that pump the trap religiously and have never jetted the line are the most common emergency call in the category. Pumping the trap and cleaning the line are two different jobs on two different schedules.

Snaking restores flow. Jetting restores diameter.

A cable through a greased line punches a hole in the deposit. Flow comes back, the kitchen reopens, and the layer on the wall is still there, which is why the same line backs up again in weeks. High-pressure water scours the wall back toward full diameter instead. For a grease line that distinction is the whole game, and it is covered properly in hydro jetting versus snaking.

Pro Jet runs trailer-mounted hydro jetting on lines from one inch up past twelve, which covers kitchen branch lines and building mains on the same visit. On older cast iron, grease is often sitting on top of scale, and the scale has to come off too or the rough surface re-coats almost immediately. Cast iron drain pipe failure explains why that surface behaves the way it does. Rates are on the pricing page: hydro jetting $450–$800, snaking $150–$300, quoted per line for commercial work after an inspection.

Set the interval from the camera, not the calendar

Every kitchen has a different grease load. A fryer-heavy menu at volume is not a bakery, and a fifty-foot run to the main is not a four-foot run. Any interval someone quotes you without looking at your line is a guess dressed up as a standard.

The way to get a real number is to clean the line, camera it clean, then camera it again at an interval and look at how much wall coating came back. That tells you your actual rate of deposit, and the schedule follows from it. Clean too often and you are paying for nothing; clean too rarely and you are buying an emergency. The same logic runs through how often a drain line should be cleaned, and it is how the commercial maintenance programs are built: inspect, recommend a cadence, perform, then camera again to verify.

What goes wrong when there is no schedule

  • Backups at the worst hour. Grease lines fail under peak load, which by definition is during service.
  • Floor drains as the relief point. The lowest opening in the building is where a blocked line comes out. In a kitchen that is the floor, which means a closed kitchen and a cleanup.
  • Repeat callouts on the same line. The signature of cabling a greased line instead of scouring it.
  • Hidden pipe damage. Grease hides what is under it. A line that keeps grabbing can have an offset joint or a belly behind the deposit, which cleaning will never fix. That is what the camera inspection after a cleaning is for, and if the pipe itself has gone, sewer line repair and replacement is the answer rather than a tighter cleaning schedule.
  • Surprise at a health or wastewater inspection. Grease programs are enforced locally, and a line nobody has looked at is a bad place to be when somebody asks for records.

Questions

Restaurant Grease Line Maintenance FAQ

How often should a restaurant grease line be jetted?
It depends on your grease load and the length of the run, and the honest way to find out is to measure it. We clean the line, camera it clean, then camera it again later and look at how much coating came back. The interval follows from your line, not from a calendar.
We pump our grease trap on schedule. Is that enough?
No. The trap protects the public sewer. It does nothing for the pipe between your fixtures and the trap, which is the run that coats with grease. Those are two separate jobs.
Can you jet a line while we are open?
Usually the work is scheduled outside service hours so the kitchen is not down. For a line that is already backing up, we run 24/7 including holidays.
Do you clean cast iron lines with scale as well as grease?
Yes. Cast iron descaling is part of commercial work. Grease sitting on scale will re-coat in weeks if the scale is left, so on older lines both come off together.
What does commercial jetting cost?
Hydro jetting runs $450 to $800 and snaking $150 to $300. Commercial work is quoted per line after a camera inspection, because line size, length and access change the job.
