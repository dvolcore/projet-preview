Sewer Camera Inspection, Explained Step by Step

A camera inspection is how a guess becomes a diagnosis. It is also how you check a recommendation before you pay for excavation. Here is what the equipment does and how to read what comes back.

The equipment

A self-levelling camera head with its own LED lighting rides on a semi-rigid push cable fed in through a cleanout. A monitor shows live video with a footage counter, so every finding is tied to a measured distance from the access point. A sonde, a small transmitter, sits in the camera head and emits a signal that a handheld locator picks up from the surface. That is what converts a finding on screen into a mark on the ground with a depth.

Self-levelling matters more than it sounds. Because the image stays upright, you can tell the bottom of the pipe from the top. That is how standing water is distinguished from a sag, and how corrosion at the bottom of a cast iron line is distinguished from a shadow.

How the inspection runs

  1. Find access. A cleanout is best. Pulling a toilet or entering through a roof vent is possible but limits reach and what can be seen.
  2. Clear enough to see. A camera cannot see through a full blockage or deep standing water. On a blocked line the order is usually clear first, then inspect, so the camera reads the pipe instead of the debris.
  3. Push and read. The head is advanced along the line with the footage counter running.
  4. Locate. At each defect the sonde is located from grade and the point is marked with its depth.
  5. Document. Findings are recorded with footage, defect type and location.

What the camera shows, and what each finding means

  • Root intrusion. Fine hair-like roots or a dense mat at a joint. Means there is an opening in the pipe at that point.
  • Offset joint. A step in the pipe wall where one section has shifted relative to the next. Catches solids.
  • Belly or sag. Water that remains standing after flow stops. This is a geometry problem, not a clog, and cleaning will not fix it.
  • Crack or fracture. Longitudinal cracks suggest crushing load. Circumferential cracks suggest bending or settlement.
  • Scale and tuberculation. Rough, nodular orange-brown buildup in cast iron, reducing diameter and catching paper.
  • Grease. A smooth tan or white coating on the wall.
  • Collapse. Loss of pipe shape, visible soil, and the camera cannot pass.
  • Protruding or unsealed tap. A branch connection cut in badly. A frequent root entry point and a reliable place for solids to hang up.
  • Deformation. An oval cross-section, typical of Orangeburg pipe starting to fail.

What a camera cannot do

It cannot see through water or a blockage, it cannot see outside the pipe, it cannot measure remaining wall thickness, and it cannot tell you how many years a pipe has left. It tells you the condition of the pipe wall today, at a measured location. That is enough to decide between cleaning and repair, which is the decision that matters.

What you should get out of it

For every defect: a footage number, the defect type, the depth, and a surface location. Plus a clear statement of whether the recommendation is cleaning or repair, and why. If a contractor recommends excavation without showing you footage and a located point, ask for both before you agree to anything.

If you are buying an older house, or one with mature trees over the sewer path, a camera inspection before closing is the cheapest way to find out whether the lateral is a known future expense. Questions

Camera Inspection Explained FAQ

Do you need a cleanout to run a camera?
A cleanout is the best access and makes a full inspection straightforward. Without one, access through a pulled toilet or a roof vent is possible but limits how much of the line can be reached.
Can a sewer camera find a leak?
It can find the defect that is leaking, such as a crack, an open joint or a hole. It cannot see water moving in the soil outside the pipe.
Should I have the sewer cameraed before buying a house?
On an older home, or one with large trees near the sewer path, it is worth doing. A failing lateral is one of the few major house expenses that is completely invisible during a standard inspection.
Do you camera the line after cleaning it?
Yes, and that is the point of doing it. Cleaning restores flow. The camera afterwards tells you whether the pipe behind the clog is sound or defective.
