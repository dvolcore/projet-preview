Why the timing is the whole problem

A sewer finding is not unusually expensive by the standards of a house. What makes it dangerous to a sale is when it shows up. It arrives inside the inspection period, in a report the seller did not commission, read aloud by somebody whose job is to get a concession. There is no time to get a second opinion, no time to separate a cleaning problem from a repair problem, and no time to collect bids. So the seller negotiates against the largest number in the room.

The buried lateral gets missed until that moment because it is outside a standard home inspection. The InterNACHI Residential Standards of Practice excludes underground items and wastewater treatment systems, which is why the line can pass through a general inspection untouched and then turn up in a separate scope a week later.

The three outcomes, and what each one buys you

A pre-listing camera inspection has exactly three endings, and all three are better known early.

  1. The line is sound. You now own footage of a clear, full-diameter pipe, taken on a date you can point to. When a buyer's scope comes back with an opinion you disagree with, you have something to put beside it.
  2. The line needs cleaning. Grease, scale or soft buildup narrowing the pipe. This is the cheapest outcome to deal with and the most expensive one to be surprised by, because a narrowed line photographs like a failing line. Cleaning it before listing removes the argument. Our rates are on the pricing page: snaking $150–$300, hydro jetting $450–$800.
  3. The line needs repair. An offset joint, a crack, a belly, a collapse. Now you have a decision rather than an ambush: repair it and list it repaired, price it in deliberately, or disclose it and let the market respond. All three are survivable. The version where you learn about it eleven days before closing is the one that kills deals.

What makes the report useful to a listing agent

A finding is only as good as what you can hand someone. Ask for the video, a written note for each defect with its footage distance from the access point and its depth, the location marked on the ground, and a plain statement of whether the fix is cleaning or repair. That last line is the one that does the work, because the gap between "this line needs jetting" and "this line needs excavation" is the gap a buyer's agent will otherwise fill on their own.

If the recommendation is repair, get the repair scoped the same way: what gets dug, how much of the pipe gets replaced, what the yard looks like after. Sewer line repair and full replacement with excavation is work we do ourselves, so the camera finding and the repair quote come from the same crew rather than from an inspector who hands you a referral and walks.

Disclosure is a question for your broker, not your plumber

Once you have a report, you know something about the property, and what you are obliged to do with that knowledge is a question for your broker or a real estate attorney. We are a drain and sewer company. We will tell you accurately what the camera showed, in writing, and we will not tell you how to handle a disclosure. Anyone in our trade who offers you an opinion on that is out of their lane.

What we will say is that the three outcomes above are all easier to disclose than to defend. A seller who says "the line was cleaned in March, here is the footage" is in a different conversation than a seller reacting to a stranger's video.

Timing it against the listing calendar

The useful window is before photos, not after. If cleaning is needed you want it done before the yard is staged. If repair is needed you want the excavation and the sod to have happened weeks before anyone walks through. On a vacant house this is easy. On an occupied one it is a half-day of access.

Two practical notes. Access first: a scope runs best through an exterior cleanout, and if the property does not have one, that is worth knowing before a buyer's inspector discovers it. And water second: a camera cannot read a pipe through deep standing water or a full blockage, so a line that is already slow has to be cleared enough to see. That is why drain cleaning and inspection are often the same visit rather than two.

Questions

Sewer Inspection Before Listing FAQ

Is a pre-listing sewer inspection worth it on a newer house?
On a house young enough to be plumbed in PVC with no mature trees over the line, it is a judgment call. On clay tile, cast iron, or anything with a big tree near the sewer path, it answers a question a buyer is going to ask anyway.
What does a pre-listing sewer scope cost?
It is quoted per property. Call (816) 506-6243 and we will give you the number for that address.
If the scope finds a problem, do I have to fix it?
That is a decision for you and your broker, not for us. Our job is to tell you accurately what the pipe looks like and what it would take to fix. What you do with that is yours.
Can you clean the line and camera it in the same visit?
Usually, and often that is the right order. A camera cannot read a pipe through a full blockage or deep standing water, so clearing enough to see comes first.
Do you work with listing agents directly?
Yes. We take the call from whoever has the keys, and we send the written finding to whoever needs it. We do not pay for referrals, and we do not offer agents anything of value for sending us work. What we offer is the work itself, on a timeline that fits an inspection window.
