The one system a home inspection leaves out

A general home inspection is a visual inspection of what can be reached and seen. A buried sewer lateral is neither. The InterNACHI Residential Standards of Practice, the standard a great many home inspectors work to, puts underground items outside the scope of the inspection and excludes wastewater treatment systems outright. That is not a knock on inspectors. It is the definition of the job. It does mean that the pipe carrying everything out of the house is usually the one component a buyer closes on having never seen.

That gap matters more here than it would in a ten-year-old subdivision. A lot of housing stock around Kansas City, Independence and Raytown is old enough to be plumbed in clay tile or cast iron, and each fails in its own predictable way: clay at the joints, cast iron from the inside out along the bottom of the pipe. Put a mature tree over the sewer path and you have the two most common findings in the metro sitting in one yard.

What actually happens on site

A self-levelling camera head with its own lighting is fed into the line, normally through an exterior cleanout, and pushed toward the city main while a footage counter runs. A transmitter in the camera head lets a handheld locator find the head from the surface, so a defect on the monitor becomes a mark in the grass with a depth. The camera inspection page covers the equipment, and the step-by-step guide covers how a reading is taken.

Two practical points decide how good the inspection is. The first is access. A cleanout outside the house is the best entry and lets the whole lateral be run. Without one, entry through a pulled toilet or a roof vent is possible but limits reach. The second is water. A camera cannot see through a full blockage or deep standing water, so on a line that is already backing up the honest order is to clear enough to see, then inspect. If the seller's line is blocked on the day, say so in writing rather than calling the inspection complete.

Reading the footage like a buyer

You are not trying to become a technician. You are trying to sort what you see into three buckets: maintenance, future expense, and deal problem.

  • Grease or soft buildup. A smooth coating narrowing the pipe. Maintenance. Cleaning restores the diameter.
  • Scale in cast iron. Rough orange-brown nodules catching paper. Usually maintenance, sometimes the first sign that the pipe wall is going.
  • Fine root hairs at a joint. Means there is an opening. Cleaning buys time; the opening stays.
  • A dense root mass. The joint has moved or the pipe has cracked. Future expense at minimum.
  • Offset joint. A visible step in the pipe wall. Solids hang on it forever. Repair, not cleaning.
  • Belly or sag. Water standing after flow stops. A geometry problem. No amount of cleaning fixes it.
  • Crack or fracture. How serious depends on direction and width. Needs a judgment call from whoever ran the camera.
  • Oval cross-section. Classic Orangeburg deformation. That pipe is at the end of its life.
  • Collapse. Soil visible, camera cannot pass. Deal problem.

The distinction that moves money is cleaning versus repair. A line full of grease and a line with an offset joint can look similarly ugly on a monitor and are an order of magnitude apart in cost. Our jetting versus snaking guide explains why one restores flow and the other restores diameter, and sewer line repair and full replacement with excavation covers what happens when neither is enough.

What a scope cannot tell you

It cannot see outside the pipe, so it cannot find a leak in the soil. It cannot measure how much wall thickness is left. It cannot put a number of years on a pipe. Anyone who tells you a line has eleven years left is guessing. What a scope can do is tell you the condition of the pipe wall today, at a measured distance and a known depth, which is exactly what a negotiation needs.

What to ask for before you pay

Ask for four things, and ask before the truck leaves: the video, a written finding for each defect with its footage distance and depth, the surface location marked, and a plain statement of whether the recommendation is cleaning or repair and why. If a report recommends excavation without a located point and footage to back it, that is not a finding, it is a quote. One more thing worth knowing as a buyer: plenty of inspection outfits are inspection-only. They will hand you a report that ends with "have a licensed plumber repair this" and leave you to find one while the clock on your inspection period runs. Questions

Sewer Scope Before Buying FAQ

Should I get a sewer scope on every house?
On a newer house with no trees near the sewer path it is a judgment call. On an older house, a house with mature trees over the line, or any house where the seller mentions past backups, it is the cheapest way to find out whether a major expense is already sitting in the yard.
Does a sewer scope cost extra on top of the home inspection?
It is separate work with separate equipment, so yes, it is quoted separately. Call (816) 506-6243 for the number on your address.
Can the scope be done if the house is vacant and the water is off?
Usually yes. The camera does not need the fixtures running to read the pipe. It does need access, and it cannot see through deep standing water, so a line that is already blocked has to be cleared enough to inspect.
What if the seller already has a report?
Read it the same way you would read any report: footage numbers, depths, located points, and a clear cleaning-or-repair call. If those are missing, you have a summary rather than a finding.
Do you repair what you find?
Yes. Pro Jet does drain cleaning, hydro jetting, camera inspection, sewer line repair and full replacement with excavation, so there is no second contractor to bring in mid-deal.
