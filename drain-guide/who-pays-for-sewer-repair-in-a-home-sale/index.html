Who Pays for a Sewer Repair in a Home Sale?

There is no rule that assigns a sewer repair to the buyer or the seller. The contract and the negotiation decide it. What a camera report does is change what everyone is negotiating about.

The honest answer first

Nobody owes you a sewer repair by default. In a residential sale the cost lands wherever the purchase contract and the parties put it, which means the real answer to "who pays" is "whoever agrees to." We are a drain and sewer company, not your agent and not your attorney, so this guide stays on the part we actually know: what the pipe is doing, what it costs to fix, and how the quality of the report changes the conversation.

Who orders the scope is a separate question from who pays for the repair. In most transactions the buyer arranges and pays for a sewer scope as part of due diligence, because the buyer is the one who wants to know. A seller can also commission one before listing, which is a different strategy with different advantages, covered in sewer inspection before you list.

The outcomes you actually see

When a scope comes back with a finding, the resolution is almost always one of these. None of them is the default; they are just the shapes the negotiation tends to take.

  • Seller repairs before closing. The work is done and verified while the seller still owns the house. The buyer gets a repaired line and footage of it. The seller keeps control of who does the work.
  • Closing credit. The seller credits an agreed amount and the buyer handles the repair after closing. The buyer picks the contractor and carries the risk that the real cost exceeds the credit.
  • Price reduction. Economically similar to a credit, treated differently in the contract and by a lender. Your agent will have a view on which fits.
  • Split. Both sides contribute, usually when the finding is genuinely ambiguous or the pipe was always going to need work eventually.
  • Buyer accepts it. The buyer takes the line as-is, knowingly, usually because the finding is maintenance rather than failure.

Which of those is available depends on the contract you signed, your inspection and resolution deadlines, and in some jurisdictions on local rules about private sewer laterals at transfer. Those are questions for your agent, your broker or a real estate attorney. We do not give that advice and you should be wary of a contractor who does.

Where a camera report changes the number

Here is the part that is in our lane, and it is the part that moves the most money.

A vague finding gets negotiated at the price of the worst thing it could be. "The sewer line has issues" invites a buyer to price in a full replacement, because nothing in the sentence rules one out. A specific finding gets negotiated at the price of the actual repair. "Offset joint at 41 feet, four and a half feet deep, in the front yard, clay tile, everything else sound" is a defined job with a defined cost.

The same logic cuts the other way for a buyer. A seller saying "it just needs a cleaning" is an opinion until there is footage showing a sound pipe wall under the deposit. The distinction between a line that needs cleaning and a line that needs repair or replacement is the difference between our published cleaning rates and an excavation, and it is decided by what the pipe wall looks like once the camera can see it. Hydro jetting versus snaking explains why a cleaned line and a repaired line are not the same claim.

What makes a report usable in a negotiation

Four things, and a report without them is a summary rather than a finding:

  1. Footage distance from the access point for every defect, so it can be found again.
  2. Depth and a surface location, marked, so the scope of any excavation is known rather than estimated.
  3. The defect named. Root intrusion, offset joint, belly, crack, scale, grease, collapse. Each one has a different fix and a different price. The camera inspection guide covers how each reads on screen.
  4. A cleaning-or-repair recommendation, with the reason. This is the sentence a negotiation turns on.

And one structural point worth raising before you choose who runs the camera: a lot of scope providers are inspection-only. Their report ends with a recommendation to hire a plumber, which means the moment the finding exists, both sides start hunting for a repair bid with a deadline running. We run the camera and do the repair, so the finding and the price for fixing it come from the same visit. On a transaction timeline that is often worth more than anything else about the inspection.

Questions

Who Pays for a Sewer Repair FAQ

Is the seller legally required to repair a sewer line before closing?
Not by any general rule we can point you to. It depends on your purchase contract and on local requirements that can apply when a property changes hands. Ask your agent, your broker or a real estate attorney. We can tell you what the pipe needs, not who owes it.
Who pays for the sewer scope itself?
Most commonly the buyer, as part of due diligence. Sellers and listing agents also order them before listing. Either way it is quoted per property, so call (816) 506-6243 for the number on the address.
Is a closing credit or a seller-completed repair better?
They carry different risks. A completed repair means the work is done and verified before closing. A credit lets the buyer choose the contractor but leaves the buyer holding the difference if the job costs more than the credit. Which fits your deal is a question for your agent.
Can a cleaning settle it instead of a repair?
Sometimes, and that is exactly what the camera decides. Grease, soap and scale are deposits, and cleaning restores the diameter. An offset joint, a belly or a crack is a shape problem, and cleaning will not touch it.
Can you get a line scoped and repaired inside an inspection period?
That is the normal case for us, and it is why we do both. Call as early in the window as you can and we will tell you straight what is possible on that timeline.
